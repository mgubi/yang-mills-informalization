1. Main conclusions

N2 remains the closest inspected match for the particular ultraviolet density representation; the gauge analytic core has stronger direct antecedents in Bałaban, and several apparently distinctive polymer devices are already in Gawędzki–Kupiainen. The match to Sharp mass bounds for the two-dimensional O(4) model (N2), with developments in the canonical O(3) paper (N3), concerns a conjunction of formulas. It does not establish that the proof's ideas as a whole are closer to the NLSM papers. The five 1985 gauge papers directly supply nonlinear averages, controlled changes of gauge, analytic stationary backgrounds and regular background propagators. Magnen–Rivasseau–Sénéor supplies a further substantial four-dimensional gauge UV construction. Neither those antecedents nor the NLSM comparison supplies the exact stated all-composite, phase, stress and OPE endpoint. The converse description, “essentially Balaban, with the remainder just formalization,” is also unsupported. Confidence is high in the individual formula matches and older antecedents, medium in ranking the closest complete representation, and low in claims of worldwide novelty.

Four observations determine this verdict.

  1. The exact density format is unusually close to N2. The clipped kinetic term, masked square, regular errors with subtraction conditions, compulsory covering inventories, fixed reference thresholds, window inverse columns and exact reset occur together in N2 §5, pp.38–55. 1.1 Scope and vocabulary

Claims about the proof's mechanisms rest on the Lean definitions and proofs cited below. The statement.md, summary.md and unit outlines provide orientation; their assertions about the literature are not evidence for these comparisons. The counts concern declarations used by the main theorem, as identified by the existing dependency information. This is a comparison of mathematical mechanisms, not a certification of the Lean theorem or of the six preprints.

“Same idea/device” means a fairly specific construction with the same role and structural constraints. “Same general method” means an established technique compatible with many implementations. “Superficial resemblance” means common terminology or a common goal without a matched mechanism. Those distinctions matter particularly for the words sector, Gaussian, annulus, locality, nonfree and continuum.

The classifications used below are:

  • STANDARD: widely established mathematics or textbook physics, including routine functional analysis.

  • ADAPTED-BALABAN: a specific gauge/RG mechanism closely matching the inspected Balaban programme.

  • ADAPTED-NLSM: a specific mechanism most closely matching one of N1–N6 in the sources read.

  • ADAPTED-OTHER: an identified older method outside those two groups.

  • candidate-NEW: a specific combination, estimate or extension for which no matching construction was found; this is a bounded novelty claim, not established priority across the literature.

A mixture's proportions describe rough mechanism weight within that component, rounded to broad increments. They are judgement calls, not fractions of proved originality; changing them by 15–20 percentage points is often reasonable. For a split component, the reported size is its parent unit's allocation unless an actual file size is stated. Repeated parent sizes must not be added. Confidence refers to the correspondence; confidence in universal novelty is lower throughout.

2. The source toolkits

2.1 OpenAI's six papers

The papers are distinct and must not be treated as a single “NLSM method”. The following descriptions concern their stated results and actual constructions; these descriptions do not certify those results.

N1: exponential decay, not an exact ultraviolet RG construction

Theorem 1.1, p.1, bounds the spin two-point function on every finite free-boundary subgraph of the square lattice, uniformly when ferromagnetic bond strengths are weakened or deleted. The result does not assert a full transfer gap; p.2 explicitly separates spin correlations from invariant observable sectors. Its distinctive analytic input is a fourth variation in two noncommuting rotation directions, Proposition 2.2, pp.5–8, Eq.(2.8). The finite-graph Ward identities permit arbitrary prescribed boundary spins. The commutator calculation is explicit: Eq.(2.10) is a change of variables, Eq.(2.11) gives the quadratic commutator field, and Eq.(2.12) cancels the fourth-order mixed coefficient for generator pattern \((a, b, a, b)\). The summed fourth derivative has a nonnegative-square contribution; it is not a perturbative effective-action calculation. Section 3, pp.9–12, uses signed frequency families on annuli: the commutator response gains \((\log N)^2\), whereas the error has size \(\log N\). The boundary twist partition-function ratio tends to one, uniformly under deleting bonds and vertices. Section 4 converts this into rare embedded-Ising sign-cluster crossings. Simultaneous boundary pinning allows disjoint annuli to be compared independently; a coarse-path count then gives exponential decay. The final reduction from n components to three conditions the remaining coordinates and uses uniformity under weaker bonds. Provenance implication: attributing the YM ultraviolet machinery to N1 on the basis of “non-Abelian fluctuations” would be a superficial resemblance. The N2/N3 retained-density constructions are the relevant comparators for that machinery.

N2: the principal direct comparator for the ultraviolet engine

The result, Theorem 1.1, p.3, is a full lattice transfer-gap bound of order \(\sqrt{\beta} \hspace{0.17em} e^{- \pi \beta}\). It is a lattice theorem; §11, p.81, separately discusses assumptions needed for a continuum spectral interpretation. Section 2, pp.9–11, turns a small rectangular partition-function defect into a full transfer-gap estimate by positivity and time/space doubling. Section 3, pp.12–33, proves preliminary all-observable mixing using quaternion current identities, stiffness reduction, disagreement propagation, and conditional Ising/rotator comparisons. This preliminary infrared input is logically separate from exact weak-coupling blocking. Section 4, pp.34–38, constructs scalar block precisions, conditional means and covariances, and a positive edge-stack identity. Section 5, pp.38–55, gives the exact nonlinear map, including rough configurations, and its comparison estimates. Section 6, pp.56–61, fixes the kinetic drift through finite-volume coefficient comparisons. Section 7, pp.61–65, admits trajectories and shoots to a fixed terminal kinetic coupling. Section 8, pp.65–77, compares complete positive densities with signed mandatory covering expansions. Section 9, pp.77–79, transfers a preliminary finite-box gap test from a fixed reference cutoff to all finer cutoffs. Section 10, pp.79–81, obtains an upper mass bound from correlated block observables. Appendices A–D supply the uniform free estimates, a fixed coefficient norm, complete supports of local sector factors, and joint Gaussian product majorants.

The most useful distinctive devices for comparison are these:

  1. Tagged normalized observation, Eq.(5.2), p.39: use the normalized weighted spin mean on its good branch and a weighted constituent spin on its fallback branch; normalize the output noise exactly.

  2. Clipped-plus-masked-square kinetic energy, Eq.(5.3), p.39: a quadratic direct term below threshold, a linear direct term above it, and a masked localized quadratic term.

  3. Fixed reference thresholds, Eq.(5.1), p.38: all geometry uses \(H_j\), while the actual running coupling varies in a band around it.

  4. Complete read supports, pp.39–41 and Appendix C: record masks, eligibility tests, negative queries, windows, and lifted winding paths, not merely visible field arguments.

  5. Two activity classes, Eqs.(5.7)–(5.10), pp.40–41: differentiable regular errors with zero affine Hessian; merely measurable signed large-gradient weights with nonempty inventories.

  6. Mandatory exact cover, Eq.(5.9), p.40: disjoint supports must cover precisely the bad-edge set. This is not an ordinary optional polymer gas.

  7. One Gaussian for a fixed sector, Eqs.(5.18)–(5.21), p.44: window inverse columns, symmetrized finite-range covariance, explicit density-ratio and determinant residuals.

  8. Core reserves and protector supports, Lemma 5.2, pp.43–44; Appendix C: energy per primary seed pays entropy, bad-region normalization, and derivatives through hard inputs.

  9. Joint products before expansion, Lemma 5.3, pp.47–48; Appendix D: overlapping factors use the same Gaussian; endpoint quadratic loads are controlled collectively.

  10. Inventory-preserving connected reassembly, §5.4, p.49: logarithm only for empty-inventory components; forbidden background polymers are opened and attached to seeded components.

  11. Exact kinetic reset, Eqs.(5.40)–(5.42), pp.54–55: remove constant/affine Hessian pieces and put mask-complement corrections into the compulsory gas.

  12. Cutoff matching before comparison, §7: the running marginal coordinate must agree before contracting all other endpoint data.

N3: extends the engine to continuum fields and stronger comparisons

N3 directly states the inherited scalar and density ingredients in §§3–4; §3.3, pp.23–28, explicitly defines the adapted retained-density class. Its target is \(S^2\), not the quaternion group \(S^3\); this changes the rotation algebra, physical tangent dimension, Jacobian, and drift. Theorem 1.1, pp.5–6, uses susceptibility and second-moment length to normalize the full continuum limit along all diverging real bare couplings. Theorem 1.2, pp.6–7, first constructs the theory along a terminal-coupling trajectory. Section 2, pp.9–21, proves preliminary mixing with a strict saving in \(e^{(4 \pi - \eta) \beta}\). Section 4, pp.29–39, includes ordinary and inclined first observations, their free kernels, and common history estimates. Section 5, pp.39–55, strengthens the regular contraction towards \(L^{- 2}\) using inversion/tangent-reflection cancellations. Its ambient completion, Lemma 5.2, pp.42–43, adjoins a normalized scalar Gaussian to the two-dimensional tangent field, producing an \(\mathbb{R}^3\)-valued common Gaussian without adding a physical degree of freedom. The scalar normalization cancels one color in the determinant, p.44. Sections 6–7, pp.55–69, shoot the terminal coupling and establish an integrated comparison against one positive reference law. Proposition 7.1, p.61, allows the comparison activities to be signed or complex; only the base density is required to be positive and reflection positive. Lemmas 7.3–7.4, pp.62–65, combine chessboard exponential moments, local Lipschitz fillings of rough regions, cap entropy, and retained energy gain. The covering identities keep the original region geometry fixed during replacements, §7.4, p.65. Section 8, pp.69–74, transfers a fixed-box estimate to a physical mass gap uniform in cutoff. Section 9, pp.74–82, carries independent local complex spin sources; normalizing their aligned linear response gives the product of source-normalization factors. Sections 10–11 construct Schwinger distributions, restore Euclidean rotations, reconstruct, and prove a separated nonzero fourth cumulant. Sections 12–15 remove the bounded bare-offset ambiguity. The key additional ingredients are summable sensitivity of the kinetic error (Lemma 12.2, pp.97–98) and two incommensurable offset periods arising from ordinary and inclined blockings (§15.2, pp.118–119). Not transferable without argument: these last uniqueness, mass-gap, and rotation mechanisms do not appear merely from recognizing the common ultraviolet density class.

N4: isolated mass from a massive continuum input

Theorem 1.1, p.2, asserts a positive isolated lowest atom of the spin-field Källén–Lehmann measure. The paper takes the constructed O3 field and its cutoff-uniform physical gap and factorial moments as inputs; Proposition 2.1, pp.4–5, records them. Section 3, pp.6–9, derives cylinder traces and torus-to-plane comparisons. Section 4, pp.9–14, bounds covariance of even spin polynomials quadratically in spin two-point correlations, separating even and odd transfer sectors. Section 5, pp.14–19, attaches quantitatively visible spin weight to low-energy states through embedded sign clusters and circuits. Section 6, pp.19–21, obtains a linear-in-circumference state count below the two-excitation threshold using trace doubling and positive polynomial energy cutoffs. Section 7, pp.21–25, combines momentum windows with the state count to prove that only finitely many masses lie near the bottom of spectral support. The proof does not obtain a particle pole from exponential decay alone. Provenance implication: its use of positive spectral measures is standard shared machinery; its two-dimensional cluster/circuit/state-count mechanism is not established as a YM antecedent by the inspected Lean.

N5: an exact mass via a second regulator

Theorem 1.1, p.3, states the exact prefactor \(32 e^{\pi / 4 - 1 / 2}\) for the full O4 transfer gap in original lattice time units. Sections 2–3, pp.6–16, compare the spin-visible mass with the full spectrum and a rectangular partition-function rate. Sections 4–6, pp.16–55, introduce a Brownian U(2) regulator, a particle expansion, a commuting contact Hamiltonian, integral equations, finite-circle Bethe states and a spectral maximization over particle parities. These are distinctive integrability and regulator-specific steps; no basis was found for importing them into the YM comparison. Sections 7–9, pp.55–97, adapt the retained-density engine to four kinetic coefficients: spatial and temporal stiffnesses for each of the quaternion and circle fields, a real circle lift, complex weights, and integer curvature. The observation in §7.4, p.61, uses normalized mean/fallback noise on flat blocks and a medoid with randomized symmetric ties when curvature is present. Section 7.7, pp.65–68, retains the same window Gaussian, determinant residuals and joint-factor logic. Section 9.2, pp.87–88, shoots four parameters rather than a single coupling. Sections 9.3–9.6 compare the possibly complex Brownian expression by integration against the positive flat reference measure, including growing rectangular aspect ratios. Section 10, pp.98–104, determines a finite coupling shift by a helicity response, separately from the partition-error estimate. Section 11, pp.104–107, extracts odd/even traces, removes the additional circle field, and transfers the auxiliary mass. Provenance implication: the generalized exact-density engine is relevant; Bethe states, U(2) loops, winding-sector parity and exact mass extraction are not evidenced as ingredients of this Lean construction.

N6: XY uses a different mechanism

Theorem statements in §1, pp.2–4, concern the critical spin field as an imaginary exponential of a Dirichlet GFF, normalized by exact center magnetization and a diagonal Green factor. Section 2, pp.6–12, imports critical height and pin limits and relates spins to magnetic height sectors. Section 3, pp.12–20, proves fixed-positive-radius-hole sector comparisons by Gaussian variational estimates. Section 4, pp.20–24, controls flat pins on long annuli. Section 5, pp.24–28, glues conditional interface kernels while summing the integer constant mode. Lemma 5.4, p.27, is a useful specific device: kernels in \((0, 1]\) with small average loss contract centered densities, so an arbitrarily poor but positive initial core eventually produces a last marginal bounded by two. Section 6, pp.28–35, cancels identical microscopic annular normalizations against the center observable and obtains separated correlations. Section 7, pp.35–43, uses Lee–Yang moments, integrable two-point bounds, collision removal and local Sobolev tightness. Provenance implication: this is not the retained-action/kinetic-reset construction of N2–N3. Calling any annulus or Gaussian comparison in YM an XY adaptation would be superficial without further evidence.

2.2 Balaban's gauge toolkit

Balaban's programme starts with compact-group link variables and preserves a complete effective density while integrating short scales. Its main analytic tasks are gauge fixing, regular localized propagators, nonlinear background fields, marginal extraction, and control of large-field regions through many iterations. The pertinent sources span 1983–1989; they are not a single theorem that can be invoked as an unconditional four-dimensional construction.

Block averaging and gauge fixing. B-Prop-I §A, pp.18–20, Eqs.(1.8)–(1.17), gives contour averages and their linear versions after axial fixing. B-RG-I pp.252–254, Eqs.(0.4)–(0.15), uses route symmetrization, analytic nonlinear group means and gauge-invariant kernels. The mean respects inversion, permutation, left/right multiplication and linearization. B-Average pp.19–20 Eqs.(10)–(15), Proposition 3 p.36 and Proposition 7 p.43 directly develop nonlinear averages, quadratic remainders and analytic background dependence. B-Gauge Theorem 2 p.83 and Proposition 7 p.100 establish controlled axial/Landau gauge transfer and its inverse. Thus nonlinear averaging, gauge reconciliation and regular slices are older mechanisms; neither symmetry averaging nor a soft normalized kernel distinguishes N2 by itself. B-Prop-I §C, pp.20–26, changes to a divergence-gauge formulation with projection operators to obtain regular propagators. The Lean comparison is YM/RG/Patch.lean:102–179, YM/RG/Block.lean:49–72, and YM/PCalc/Chart.lean:52–67; the formulas and division of gauge roles differ.

Variational problem and backgrounds. The nonlinear stationary background is built from a linear lift plus corrections satisfying local analytic equations. B-RG-II §1, pp.2–3, Eqs.(1.2)–(1.5), recalls those equations and localizes their propagators. B-Var directly supplies the gauge comparison: Theorem 1 p.279 gives the unique small critical orbit and regularity; Proposition 6 pp.295–296 gives the analytic small solution; Proposition 9 p.309 gives dependence on coarse averages and external background, with localized derivative bounds (190), p.308. Its fixed-average constrained problem differs from the Lean soft-kernel problem. The derivative calculations preceding (189) are expressly omitted on p.308; this is a comparison of stated mechanisms, not certification of those estimates. YM/PCalc/Chart.lean:78–100 and YM/Push/STATIONARY_UNIFORM/ImplicitRadius.lean:24–43, YM/Push/STATIONARY_UNIFORM/ImplicitRadius.lean:80–92 implement the corresponding task for the actual soft-block tree chart.

Propagators and random walks. B-Green §2, pp.574–585, patches local inverses by generalized random-walk expansions. B-Prop-I §F, pp.35–40, applies regularity/locality estimates to gauge propagators. B-Prop-II handles irregular domains and restrictions at several scales; see p.229 and Eq.(2.86), p.238. The traced Lean free rows instead arise from analytic symbols, Laurent coefficients and finite-period Fourier identities, YM/Work/F07.lean:34–81, YM/Work/F07.lean:109–112. B-Back Theorems 3.1–3.4 pp.397–400 supplies exponential decay, derivative/Hölder regularity and analytic background dependence; p.399 makes the constants independent of the admissible domain sequence. The difference is implementation and required geometry: Balaban himself uses Fourier and Laurent formulas elsewhere.

Small/large fields and localization. B-Conv §2, pp.254–262, represents complete densities by nested region histories with coupling-dependent cutoffs. B-Large-I pp.175–177 explains why one rare-region penalty cannot simply be carried unchanged through arbitrarily many four-dimensional steps.
